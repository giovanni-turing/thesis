\chapter{The Azure Multimodal Inference Trace: An Exploratory Characterization}
\label{cap:trace-eda}
\chaptermark{The Azure Multimodal Inference Trace}

This chapter characterizes the workload dataset used throughout the thesis:
Microsoft's \textit{AzureLMMInferenceDataset2025}~\cite{AzureLMM2025}, a
production trace of a multimodal-model inference cluster released with the
ModServe paper~\cite{Qiu2025ModServe}. The analysis uses all four
schema fields and quantifies three properties that motivate
the rest of this work: vision work decoupled from request rate, the
vision signal's burst structure, and the differing predictability of each
pool's demand. All analyses ran on GPU (cuDF, cuML, CuPy; RTX~5090);
every figure and number is reproducible from versioned scripts
inventoried in the accompanying workspace report.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:eda-dataset}

The trace covers exactly seven days (2024-10-15 to 2024-10-22, UTC) and
one million requests, with four fields per request: arrival timestamp
(millisecond precision), image count, context tokens, and generated
tokens. Coverage is continuous (largest gap \SI{39}{\second}; median
inter-arrival \SI{312}{\milli\second}). The 151 fully duplicated rows
($1.5\times10^{-4}$ of the trace) are \emph{retained}
(indistinguishable from legitimate simultaneous arrivals); analyses
process ties in file order. Three structural facts shape all downstream use.

First, exactly 50.0\% of requests are text-only, a by-construction
split that must be stated when generalizing from this trace. Second,
image counts are perfect squares: 71 distinct $k^2$ values cover 99.99\%
of image-bearing requests, up to $97^2 = \num{9409}$ images in a single
request. Images therefore arrive as $k\times k$ tile or frame grids:
bulk batches, not independent single images. Third, the
context-token field \emph{includes} the traced model's vision tokens, with
a non-constant per-image increment (${\approx}277$ tokens per image at one
image, falling to ${\approx}66$ at 529): no clean additive decomposition
into text and vision tokens exists, confirmed by the dataset's official
schema documentation (Figure~\ref{fig:eda-ctx-vs-img}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figuras/eda/eda_a_context_vs_numimages}
\caption{Median context tokens conditioned on image count (image-bearing

\section{Request anatomy}
\label{sec:eda-requests}

All three request-level marginals carry large upper-tail mass relative
to their medians: ``heavy-tailed'' in the operational sense used
throughout this chapter
(Table~\ref{tab:eda-percentiles}, Section~\ref{sec:eda-globals}).
Tail-index estimates (Hill estimator over the top percentile of order
statistics; indicative values, no formal goodness-of-fit claim):
generated tokens are the heaviest (Hill ${\approx}1.9$), so decode-side
capacity is quantile- rather than mean-driven; context tokens and image
counts have tamer tails (Hill ${\approx}6.6$ and $7.2$). The top
percentile of requests alone carries 15.2\% of all context tokens and 13.4\%
of all generated tokens.
Multimodal requests have longer inputs but \emph{shorter} outputs than
text-only requests (context median 1{,}422 vs.\ 792 tokens; generation
median 80 vs.\ 147), a question-answering profile against longer-form
text chat.

Unsupervised clustering of all requests on $\log(1{+}\text{images})$,
$\log(1{+}\text{context})$, and $\log(1{+}\text{generated})$ renders
four interpretable archetypes (Table~\ref{tab:eda-archetypes},
Figure~\ref{fig:eda-clusters}). The fit is cuML $k$-means with $k{=}4$,
which maximizes the silhouette over $k \in \{3,\dots,9\}$ at $0.388$,
against $0.378$ at $k{=}3$ and $0.319$ at $k{=}5$ (50k-point evaluation
subsample, fixed seed); the separation is weak, so the groups are
interpretive rather than sharply distinct. The
video/frame-analysis archetype (3.4\% of requests)
carries 85.7\% of all image volume and a third of
all prefill tokens. It is the clustering's rendering of the
threshold-defined \emph{frames class} used elsewhere
($k{>}10$ grids: 2.7\% of requests, 81.2\% of image volume); the two
definitions overlap but are not identical, hence the slightly different
shares. Each archetype stresses a different serving
stage: video jobs the vision encoder and prefill; conversational text
decode; extraction-style requests almost nothing.

\section{Temporal structure}
\label{sec:eda-temporal}

The arrival process is not Poisson at any operationally relevant scale:
inter-arrival coefficient of variation 1.55, overdispersed per-second
counts, and a Fano factor that keeps growing across the entire measured
range of counting windows, from 1.8 at \SI{1}{\second} through 31.9 at
one minute to 938 at one hour
(Figure~\ref{fig:eda-arrival}; a seven-day record cannot establish
asymptotically unbounded growth, but within the window every aggregation
scale an autoscaler could use is overdispersed).
Spectrally, a diurnal harmonic family (\SI{24}{\hour} line with
12/8/\SI{6}{\hour} overtones; in the request signal the 12- and 8-hour
harmonics exceed the 24-hour fundamental, indicating a multi-peaked daily
profile) sits on a strong $1/f^{\beta}$ long-range-dependent background.
No sharp non-harmonic line dominates the sub-diurnal band above that
background; the few sub-diurnal peaks that surface are consistent with
sidelobes of the diurnal line in a seven-cycle record. Periods of
\SI{84}{\hour} and above are record-length artifacts of the seven-day
window rather than weekly cycles.

Table~\ref{tab:eda-persistence} summarizes the four demand signals at
one-minute resolution. Two reading notes. First, the Hurst column is
\emph{derived} from the spectral slope ($H = (1+\beta)/2$): one
estimator viewed two ways, not two corroborating measurements. Second, all rate signals stamp a request's
full token volume at its arrival minute (the trace carries no
durations). That is faithful for images and prefill, which execute near
arrival, but the decode series thereby becomes an \emph{offered-work
arrival} signal ($\dagger$) whose burstiness and persistence describe
arriving decode work, not decode occupancy (a smoothed, lagged version).

The load-bearing contrast is between the two pools' arrival signals:
the image signal is simultaneously an order of magnitude burstier than
the request signal (CV$^2$ 3.83 vs.\ 0.32 on the same one-minute clock),
further above its own mean at peak ($15.6\times$ vs.\ $4.7\times$),
longer-memoried (autocorrelation half-life 69 vs.\ \SI{9}{\minute}), and
more persistent spectrally ($H \approx 0.93$ vs.\ $0.83$). Burstiness
and forecastable structure concentrate in the same, vision-borne signal.
Across all four signals the picture is not monotone: the decode series
pairs the \emph{shortest} correlation half-life (\SI{5}{\minute}) with
the second-highest spectral persistence ($H \approx 0.91$). That
combination is plausibly manufactured by the arrival-stamping
construction ($\dagger$), which compresses each request's decode work
into one minute (whitening short lags) while leaving the slow demand
envelope intact. The
half-life and Hurst orderings therefore disagree, and no joint claim is
made on them; the thesis builds on the
images-versus-requests contrast, which every column supports.

\section{The vision signal: decoupled, bursty, single-class}
\label{sec:eda-vision}

A constant-fraction model (vision work as a fixed multiple of request
rate) explains only $R^2 = \num{0.123}$ of the image-rate series
($\alpha = 15.1$ images per request, a forced through-origin fit;
Figure~\ref{fig:eda-decouple}), while
decode load is largely carried
by request rate ($R^2 = \num{0.654}$; Pearson $r$ against request rate is
$0.36$ for images versus $0.81$ for decode). The request-rate signal
(the input of conventional autoscalers) carries most information about
decode demand, almost none about vision demand. What a scaling
policy can do with this depends on system parameters (cold-start time,
per-replica capacity, cost) outside this dataset; the dataset establishes
the information asymmetry itself.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figuras/eda/eda_d_img_vs_req_scatter}
\caption{Image rate against request rate over one-minute windows. The
best-fit constant-fraction line ($15.1$ img/req) explains only $R^2 = 0.12$:
the same request rate coincides with image rates spanning two orders of
magnitude, and the highest image-rate windows are not the highest request-rate
windows. Request rate, the signal a conventional autoscaler watches,
carries almost no information about vision load.}
\label{fig:eda-decouple}
\end{figure}

The image-rate signal exceeds its 99th percentile (\num{224}~images/s) in
101 one-minute windows; consecutive windows form 87 contiguous runs,
which merge into 18 burst episodes after extending each run to its
surrounding median crossings (runs sharing an envelope are one episode).
The six largest, plus the calmest day, form the evaluation windows of
Chapters~\ref{cap:baselines} and~\ref{cap:closedloop}
(Figure~\ref{fig:eda-windows}). The largest episode
peaks at \num{370}~images/s while the request rate peaks at only
\num{4.48} requests/s --- bursts are invisible in the request signal
(Figure~\ref{fig:eda-sevenday}).
Episode composition is homogeneous: the frames class supplies
93--96\% of the image volume of every top episode, and clustering the 18
episodes on shape and composition features isolates a single mixed-composition
outlier against an otherwise uniform population. In effect the trace
contains \emph{one burst type}: frames-class floods varying in
magnitude and duration.

Bursts compound on the prefill side. Multimodal requests carry 72.5\% of
all prefill tokens, and during burst minutes the mean prefill length is
$2.69\times$ that of calm minutes: more images and longer prefills arrive
together (window-level images--context correlation $r=0.89$). Decode is the
mirror image: during the same image bursts the generated-token rate rises only
$1.78\times$, and per-request generation \emph{shortens} (median 84
vs.\ 99 tokens). The decode pool is nearly burst-neutral while the vision
and prefill stages are not. Burst onsets are fast:
baseline-to-peak in a median of \SI{6}{\minute}
(10th percentile \SI{2}{\minute}, fastest \SI{1}{\minute}), with a median
steepest single-minute jump of \num{208}~images/s. The fastest onsets
(1--2 minutes) are comparable to or faster than replica cold-starts of
tens of seconds to
minutes. The onset time is the budget for any scaling decision; whether
it defeats reactive control is settled per policy in the closed-loop
chapters (Figure~\ref{fig:eda-onsets}).

\section{Signal predictability}
\label{sec:eda-predictability}

The image signal's 69-minute autocorrelation half-life on the raw
series (autocorrelation 0.64 at a 5-minute lag) pairs with
$H \approx 0.93$. The latter is fit as the $1/f^{\beta}$ slope over the
sub-diurnal band (10 minutes to 12 hours, clear of the diurnal
fundamental), so it reflects scale-free structure rather than the daily
cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:eda-acf}). The
image-versus-request \emph{contrast} (69 versus 9 minutes) is robust to
seasonality in any case, both signals carrying the same diurnal component.
Its
long-range dependence also suggests that purely short-memory models
(Poisson or low-order ARMA) may structurally underfit it --- a risk, not
a theorem, which Chapter~\ref{cap:predictors} tests empirically.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figuras/eda/eda_d_acf}
\caption{Autocorrelation of the two arrival signals versus lag. The image
signal (blue) retains autocorrelation $0.64$ at a five-minute lag and crosses
$0.5$ only at \SI{69}{\minute}, against \SI{9}{\minute} for the request signal
(green). The demand most invisible to request-rate monitoring is also the most
\emph{persistent}: its envelope is forecastable at the longest leads (though its
per-bin accuracy is the lowest of the four signals, precisely because it is the
burstiest; Chapter~\ref{cap:predictors}). That is the feasibility premise for the
predictive-scaling contribution.}
\label{fig:eda-acf}
\end{figure}

The demand signals are not derivable from one another under any of the
relationships tested here: constant-fraction scaling, rank
correlation, and lagged linear cross-correlation. (Nonlinear or
multivariate predictors with calendar features are not ruled out by
these tests; Chapter~\ref{cap:predictors}'s covariate experiments probe
that direction and find nearly nothing to exploit at provisioning
cadence.) At request level,
image count and generation length are independent (Spearman $-0.06$;
median generation stays at 50--140 tokens across image counts from 1 to
529). At window level, the image--generation cross-correlation peaks
sharply at lag zero with no lead. Below the one-minute scale, all signals
reach a white shot-noise floor: finer-grained inputs add noise, not
information, though the required decision frequency again depends on
system parameters this dataset lacks.

\section{Resolution sensitivity and tail regimes}
\label{sec:eda-multires}

The burstiness results depend on the observation window, differently per
signal. Table~\ref{tab:eda-multires} tracks each signal's peak at a given
window size as a fraction of its \SI{1}{\second} peak. Coarsening the
window hides the vision peaks far
faster than the request peaks: at \SI{60}{\second} a monitor still sees 37\% of
the true one-second request peak but only \textbf{3.9\%} of the one-second image
peak. A minute-resolution monitor
therefore understates the encoder pool's instantaneous surges by roughly
$25\times$, but the request rate by under $3\times$. The image
signal's excess-dispersion also decays most slowly with window size
(CV$^2$ $25.7\!\rightarrow\!3.83$ from \SI{1}{\second} to \SI{60}{\second};
Figure~\ref{fig:eda-multires}),
consistent with its long-range dependence: no practical aggregation window
recovers a well-behaved image signal.

Conditioning the request marginals on their own extremes exposes who occupies
each tail. The top-1\% context-token requests are 81.6\% multimodal with a
median of 529 images --- the large-context tail \emph{is} the frames class, not
long text prompts. The top-1\% image-count requests are 100\% multimodal, carry
a median of ${\approx}\num{36000}$ context tokens (tail-deterministic
image--context coupling), and decode only briefly (median 53 generated tokens).
By contrast the busiest 1\% of request-rate minutes carry ordinary-composition
traffic: median 1 image and median context and generation within a few percent
of the global medians. High request rate does not imply heavy per-request
work. Most sharply, of 106 hot-request and 101 hot-image
minutes (each signal's set of minutes at or above its own p99; the
request set carries six minutes tied exactly at its threshold, so
it exceeds the nominal $1\%$ of \num{10080}),
only \textbf{5} are shared (Jaccard 0.02). The two pools' extreme-load events
are essentially disjoint. This is the decoupling of
Section~\ref{sec:eda-vision} at its strongest: provisioning the
vision pool from the request signal misses precisely the moments that matter.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:eda-limitations}

The trace carries no image resolutions (counts only), no session or user
identifiers, and no latency or completion-time ground truth (it is
arrival-side only). Its seven-day window supports diurnal but not weekly
periodicity claims. The 50/50 modality split is by construction. The
context-token field embeds the \emph{traced} model's vision tokenization,
so token counts are self-consistent as a workload but not transferable to
a different model's tokenizer.

Everything in this chapter describes \emph{this} trace, one week of
one production cluster, and the thesis's generality argument runs
through mechanism, not sampling. The near-pure frames-class composition
of every major burst points to a specific mechanism: a few tenants
submitting batch video/frame
pipelines into a cluster that also serves interactive traffic. That is
consistent with the bursty, heterogeneous image traffic ModServe reports
across its fleet~\cite{Qiu2025ModServe}. Under that mechanism the
decoupling should recur wherever batch visual workloads share a serving
system with interactive chat, while its \emph{magnitude} remains
workload-specific. The claim is falsifiable: a multimodal trace in
which image volume tracks request rate (uniformly image-light requests,
or admission control that spreads frames jobs) would collapse the
decoupling, and with it the case for a separate vision-pool signal.

\section{Summary}

Half the requests are text-only, images arrive as tile/frame batches,
four archetypes stress different serving stages, and the arrival process
is non-Poisson at every scale, with diurnal harmonics over a
long-range-dependent background. The vision demand signal is at once the
least visible to request-rate monitoring ($R^2 = 0.123$), the burstiest
(CV$^2$ 3.83; single-class frames floods ramping in a median of six
minutes), and the most persistent ($H \approx 0.93$, 69-minute memory),
while decode demand, read as offered work, is largely rate-carried and
comparatively benign. Even the extremes are disjoint (5 shared minutes
of ${\approx}100$ each), and minute-resolution monitoring recovers under
4\% of the true instantaneous image peak. These are properties of the
dataset; the chapters that follow develop their consequences for
serving-system design.
